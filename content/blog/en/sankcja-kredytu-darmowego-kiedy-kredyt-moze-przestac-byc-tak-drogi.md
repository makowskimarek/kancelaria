The ‘free loan sanction’, or SKD, was for years a concept known mainly to lawyers specialising in consumer credit. Today, it is increasingly coming to the attention of the courts, the media and in discussions between borrowers and banks – and for good reason. Current case law from the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) and national courts shows that the way in which consumer credit agreements are drafted can have very serious consequences for lenders, perhaps even more so than the much-discussed issue of ‘franc-denominated’ agreements.

The SKD stems from Article 45 of the Consumer Credit Act. Put simply, this means that if a lender has breached specific information obligations, the consumer – after submitting an appropriate declaration – may repay the loan without interest or other costs due to the lender, within the timeframe and in the manner set out in the agreement. The Act also sets a time limit for exercising this right: as a general rule, it expires one year after the date of performance, i.e. full repayment, of the agreement.

This is therefore not a matter of a minor complaint concerning a single charge. If successfully pursued, an SKD claim can lead to a very significant reduction in the cost of the loan, and in the case of contracts that have already been repaid, to a claim for the refund of sums collected by the bank or lending institution.

### **The CJEU strengthens consumer protection, but does not provide an automatic remedy**

In recent months, rulings by the CJEU have been of particular significance for SKD cases. In its judgment of 13 February 2025 in Case C-472/23, the Court analysed the relationship between the penalties provided for under national law and the requirement that penalties for breaches of the Consumer Credit Directive must be effective, proportionate and dissuasive.

This judgement is significant for two reasons. Firstly, it confirms that a sanction may be severe for the lender if the breach of information obligations had a real impact on the consumer’s situation. Secondly, it serves as a reminder that a court may examine the proportionality of a penalty. In other words, the standard penalty clause (SKD) should not be treated as a mere automatic mechanism triggered by every typographical error in a contract. What matters is the nature of the breach, its significance for the consumer and its impact on the consumer’s ability to assess the obligation.

Of even greater practical significance is the judgment of the CJEU of 23 April 2026 in Case C-744/24, Bank Polska Kasa Opieki. The Court held that a bank may not charge interest on sums intended to cover costs associated with a consumer loan. Following this judgment, the Financial Ombudsman emphasised that charging interest on an amount that was never made available to the customer constitutes an improper practice that inflates the lender’s profit.

This is particularly relevant for agreements in which a commission, insurance premium or other costs have been capitalised, added to the outstanding balance and then subject to interest. In such cases, it is worth checking whether the lender has correctly stated the total loan amount, the total cost of the loan, the APR, the rules for calculating interest and the repayment schedule.

### **What are the most common points of dispute?**

In SKD cases, the most common issues examined relate to inaccuracies in the information about the costs of the loan. These include, amongst other things, an incorrect APRC, an incorrect specification of the total amount of the loan, unclear interest calculation rules, errors in the repayment schedule, the inclusion of non-interest costs in the loan amount, and a lack of a clear description of commissions, insurance or other charges.

Not every shortcoming will be sufficient grounds for a claim. Nor, however, can it be assumed that a bank or lending institution is beyond reproach simply because the agreement was signed many years ago and was a standard template used for thousands of customers. It is precisely these mass-produced contract templates that are often the source of recurring breaches.

That is why, in SKD cases, analysing the documents is crucial. The mere fact that the loan was a ‘cash loan’, a ‘consolidation loan’ or a ‘loan for any purpose’ is not enough. It is necessary to examine the contract, the information form, the repayment schedule, the table of charges, any annexes, the repayment history and the method of settling commissions and other costs.

### **The Supreme Court still has important questions to resolve**

Case law concerning SKD cases is still developing. A legal issue, registered under reference number III CZP 15/25, is pending before the Supreme Court, concerning, amongst other things, whether the court is obliged to examine of its own motion all grounds justifying the application of the ‘free loan’ sanction, or whether it is bound by the content of the borrower’s declaration. These proceedings are currently suspended, however, due to the fact that the CJEU is examining analogous preliminary questions, on which the Polish Supreme Court is awaiting a ruling. A consistent ruling from the CJEU and the Polish Supreme Court is therefore to be expected.

This is important because, in practice, the content of the statement submitted to the bank is of great significance. A poorly drafted statement may narrow the scope of the dispute, hinder the presentation of arguments, or give the bank grounds to claim that the consumer has not effectively invoked all the breaches. In SKD cases, therefore, what matters is not only whether the contract contains errors, but also how and when the consumer relies on them.

### **Toxic loans are not limited to SKD**

It is worth taking a broader view of SKD cases. The consumer credit market, much like the foreign currency loan market before it, has for years been based on complex contractual templates, high non-interest costs, insurance, commissions and structures which the average consumer was unable to properly verify before signing the contract.

That is why we are talking not only about the ‘free loan’ penalty, but more broadly about toxic loans – contracts in which the economic burden was concealed within the structure of costs, exchange rates, tables, commissions or conversion mechanisms, and which very often created a situation that was unilaterally advantageous to the lender and disadvantageous to the consumer. In the light of case law, this is controversial, to say the least.

### **What should a consumer do?**

If a consumer loan included a high commission, insurance included in the loan, an unclear repayment schedule, a suspiciously high APR, or costs added to the principal, it is worth analysing the contract. This applies to both active loans and those that have already been repaid – although in the latter case, the one-year time limit, calculated from the date of performance of the agreement, is particularly important.

However, we do not recommend automatically sending a ready-made template statement found somewhere online. The SKD is a powerful tool, but it requires precision. Before submitting a statement, it is worth determining what breaches actually exist in the contract, what claims can be made, whether there are any time-limit issues, and how the bank might respond to the complaint.
