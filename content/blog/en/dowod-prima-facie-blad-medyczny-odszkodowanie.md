## Does the patient have to prove the doctor’s negligence with 100 per cent certainty?

In cases seeking compensation for medical negligence, it is generally the patient who must demonstrate the grounds for the doctor’s or medical facility’s liability, in particular the damage suffered, the event giving rise to liability, and the causal link between them. This follows from the general principles of civil liability, including Article 6 of the Civil Code.

The problem is that in medicine, reconstructing the exact sequence of events can be extremely difficult.

A patient is not consciously present during an operation carried out under general anaesthesia, is unable to observe compliance with hygiene procedures, and usually lacks the knowledge to independently assess the correctness of medical procedures, even after the event.

Often, even medical experts are unable to unequivocally pinpoint the moment at which an infection, organ damage or other complication occurred.

If, in every such case, the patient were required to have absolute certainty regarding the course of events, it would be practically impossible to claim compensation for many genuine instances of negligence.

This is precisely why the concept of *prima facie* evidence has been accepted in case law.

## What is prima facie evidence?

The term *prima facie* comes from Latin and means ‘at first glance’. In law, however, it does not refer to a superficial assessment of the situation, nor does it mean that the patient is automatically in the right simply because complications arose following treatment.

**Prima facie evidence is a method of proving a case in which, on the basis of established facts, the principles of common experience and specialist knowledge, it can be assumed that there is a high probability of a specific sequence of events having occurred.**

For example, a patient was admitted to hospital without any symptoms of infection. During their hospital stay, they underwent surgery, following which a specific infection was diagnosed. The medical records and expert opinions indicate that the nature of the infection, the timing of its onset and the course of treatment suggest that the infection was healthcare-associated.

In such cases, the patient does not necessarily have to identify which specific member of staff performed the procedure incorrectly, nor the exact moment when the infection occurred. The court may assess the circumstances as a whole and determine whether the infection occurred, with a sufficiently high degree of probability, as a result of an event for which the healthcare facility is responsible.

However, this is not an automatic rule. Each case requires a separate assessment, and the hospital may present evidence pointing to other causes of the harm.

## Prima facie evidence and hospital-acquired infections

Infections associated with the provision of healthcare services are among the most typical examples of cases in which prima facie evidence may apply.

In the case of a post-operative infection, it is often impossible to determine unequivocally when the microorganisms entered the patient’s body. This does not, however, mean that the healthcare facility can avoid liability solely because it is impossible to reconstruct the course of the infection with precision.

In its judgment of 13 June 2000 (V CKN 34/00), the Supreme Court addressed the possibility of establishing a causal link in medical cases on the basis of high probability rather than absolute certainty — for it is not possible to impose on the claimant the unrealistic requirement of precisely demonstrating the moment and route by which the infection entered the body. Case law emphasises that a patient cannot be required to prove circumstances which, objectively speaking, cannot be established with absolute certainty.

Of particular relevance to the injured party may be: their state of health prior to hospitalisation, the results of microbiological tests, the time at which symptoms appeared, the type of procedure performed, treatment records and an expert’s opinion.

At the same time, the mere occurrence of an infection in hospital does not automatically establish the hospital’s liability. This requires the demonstration of relevant grounds, and the complication may also have other causes unrelated to the staff.

## Post-operative complications – when can it be considered medical negligence?

*Prima facie* evidence may also be relevant in cases concerning injuries sustained during or immediately after an operation.

Let us imagine a patient who had no specific neurological disorders prior to the operation, but who was found to have nerve damage after the operation. The patient does not know whether the damage resulted from incorrect positioning of the body, the use of a surgical instrument, pressure or some other treatment-related incident.

It will not always be possible to establish a single, indisputable cause.

In such cases, it may be important to examine whether the nature of the injury, its location, the timing of the onset of symptoms and the course of treatment indicate a high probability of a link between the injury and the actions of the medical staff. In its judgment of 6 February 2025 (II CSKP 2367/22), the Supreme Court addressed the significance of *prima facie* evidence in a case concerning a spinal injury following surgery. It emphasised that in medical cases, it is sometimes impossible to establish all stages of the causal link; therefore, a sufficiently high degree of probability may be sufficient.

This is an important point for patients: **the inability to unequivocally identify the technical cause of a complication does not in itself mean that a claim for damages is without merit.**

## Does *prima facie* evidence mean that the hospital must prove its innocence?

No. This is one of the most common misunderstandings surrounding this legal concept.

*Prima facie* evidence does not mean an automatic reversal of the burden of proof, nor does it imply that every complication is the result of a doctor’s negligent act.

In a significant part of case law, this concept is based on Article 231 of the Code of Civil Procedure, concerning presumptions of fact. However, there are differing views in legal scholarship as to its nature and its relationship to the burden of proof.

The court may deem certain facts to be established if they follow from other facts that have already been proven. The patient must therefore present evidence that allows such reasoning to be applied. **It is not sufficient merely to claim that, since the patient was healthy before hospitalisation and symptoms appeared afterwards, the hospital is liable for the damage.**

On the other hand, the healthcare facility may challenge the reasoning put forward by pointing to an alternative cause of the complication, unrelated to improper treatment.

The final assessment rests with the court, which analyses all the evidence gathered in the case.

## What evidence should you gather to claim compensation?

In a medical negligence case, it is particularly important to prepare the evidence properly before filing a claim with the court.

Complete medical records form the basis of this. It is worth obtaining not only the hospital treatment summary, but also documentation of the course of hospitalisation, descriptions of procedures, test results, referral forms, nursing records and documents relating to the subsequent treatment of complications.

Specialist consultations, medical opinions, rehabilitation records and information regarding the patient’s state of health prior to the incident may be no less important.

In many cases, witness statements and information enabling the chronology of events to be reconstructed are also significant.

A key element of court proceedings is usually the expert opinion of a medical specialist in the relevant field. It is this opinion that can help determine whether there is a high probability of a link between a specific medical procedure and the harm suffered.

Therefore, the litigation strategy should not be limited to making a general allegation of medical negligence. It is necessary to determine precisely which circumstances need to be proven and which questions need to be put to the experts.

## What compensation can be obtained for medical negligence?

The scope of claims depends on the type of harm, its consequences and the grounds for liability.

If the liability of a healthcare facility or doctor is established, the patient may primarily claim:

- **Compensation** for the harm, pain and suffering endured.
- **Compensation** covering reasonable costs of treatment, rehabilitation, care and other financial losses.
- **A pension**, if the harm has led, amongst other things, to increased needs, loss of earning capacity or reduced prospects for the future.
- **A finding of liability for the future consequences of the incident**, where there are legal grounds for doing so.

The amount of any potential claims depends on the individual circumstances. There is no single fixed rate for a hospital-acquired infection, nerve damage or a post-operative complication.

Prima facie evidence may make it easier to establish the grounds for liability, but does not in itself determine the amount of compensation due.

## Is it worth suing a hospital if the cause of the complications is uncertain?

The lack of a clear answer as to the cause of the harm should not automatically deter a patient from exploring the possibility of pursuing a claim.

However, a distinction must be made between a situation where there are strong indications of medical negligence and a case where the complication is a known risk of properly conducted treatment.

The mere occurrence of an adverse outcome does not imply liability on the part of the doctor or hospital.

Only an analysis of the medical records, current medical knowledge, the nature of the complication and its possible causes allows for an assessment of whether there are grounds for establishing prima facie evidence and pursuing a claim for compensation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it possible to win a medical negligence case without direct evidence?

Yes, in certain situations. The court may establish the relevant circumstances on the basis of other proven facts, provided they indicate a sufficiently high probability of a link between the treatment and the harm. However, this does not mean that a mere suspicion of medical negligence is sufficient.

### Does a hospital-acquired infection always constitute medical negligence?

No. The infection itself does not automatically establish the healthcare provider’s liability. It is necessary to establish the grounds for liability, including causation and – where required – fault. Prima facie evidence may, however, facilitate the demonstration of relevant circumstances where it is impossible to determine the exact source of the infection.

### Is an expert opinion required in a medical negligence case?

In the vast majority of cases involving specialist medical issues, an expert’s opinion is of fundamental importance. It enables an assessment of the correctness of the treatment, the possible causes of the harm and the likelihood of a causal link. A private specialist’s opinion may assist in preparing the case, but it does not automatically replace the opinion of an expert appointed by the court.

### Does the absence of information regarding the cause of a complication in the medical records preclude the possibility of obtaining compensation?

Not necessarily. Medical records are important evidence, but their absence or incompleteness does not always prevent the facts from being established on the basis of other available evidence. Test results, subsequent records, expert opinions and witness statements may all be relevant.

### Does prima facie evidence shift the burden of proof to the hospital?

Not automatically. In the commonly accepted view, it constitutes a facilitation of the burden of proof based on a high degree of probability, rather than a statutory reversal of the burden of proof. The patient should present facts enabling the court to draw the appropriate conclusions. The hospital may present evidence to challenge such reasoning.

### Is it possible to obtain compensation for post-operative complications?

Yes, if there are grounds for attributing liability to a doctor or a medical facility. However, not every complication constitutes a medical error. Among other things, it is important whether the staff acted in accordance with current medical knowledge, exercised due care, and correctly diagnosed and treated the complications.

### How long do you have to claim compensation for medical negligence?

The limitation period depends on the basis of the claim and the circumstances of the case. In the case of tort claims, Article 442¹ of the Civil Code generally applies, which provides, amongst other things, for a three-year limitation period calculated from the date on which the claimant becomes aware of the damage and the person liable to remedy it, with additional rules concerning personal injury. Therefore, the limitation period must be determined on a case-by-case basis, rather than assuming that it always runs from the date of the operation.

### Can claims be made directly against the hospital’s insurer?

In certain cases, yes. If the healthcare facility’s liability is covered by civil liability insurance, the injured party may, in principle, direct their claim directly to the insurer. However, this does not mean that the insurer will automatically accept liability or pay the amount claimed.
