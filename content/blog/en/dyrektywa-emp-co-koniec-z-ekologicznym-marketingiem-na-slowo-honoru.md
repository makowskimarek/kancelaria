Directive 2024/825, known as EmpCo, is intended to strengthen consumer protection against greenwashing and unfair practices relating to environmental claims. The Polish draft bill implementing the directive was adopted by the Council of Ministers on 7 July 2026 and provides for amendments to the Act on Combating Unfair Market Practices and the Act on Consumer Rights. The new regulations are due to come into force on 27 September 2026.

## EmpCo – what is it all about?

EmpCo is an abbreviation for Directive (EU) 2024/825 of the European Parliament and of the Council, which aims to strengthen the position of consumers in the green transition. In practice, the aim is to ensure that consumers receive more reliable information about products, and that businesses cannot gain a market advantage through general, unverified or exaggerated environmental claims.

The Directive amends two key pieces of legislation: Directive 2005/29/EC on unfair commercial practices and Directive 2011/83/EU on consumer rights. The European Commission points out that the new rules are intended to provide consumers with better information on the durability and reparability of products and to strengthen protection against greenwashing and practices involving the premature ageing of products.

From the perspective of businesses, EmpCo is therefore not just another ‘environmental’ regulation in the narrow sense. It is a change in consumer law that will affect marketing, labelling, product descriptions, environmental claims, online communication, the presentation of guarantees, information on durability and reparability, and the general way in which a product’s environmental impact is discussed.

This does not, however, mean that environmental claims are completely prohibited in all contexts. It is easy to imagine such claims being used in contexts other than environmental ones – for example, using a green leaf to highlight a product’s natural origin. According to the explanations published by the European Commission (specifically the answer to question 5 [included in the materials from June this year](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/3c257883-bb2a-4dd9-a6dc-501d587bb34f_en?filename=faq-empowerting-consumers-gtd.pdf)) is permissible under the condition set out in Directive 2005/29 – that is, provided that it does not mislead the so-called *average consumer* as to the product’s environmental merits.

## What will the Polish Act change?

The Polish draft Act transposing the provisions of the EmpCo Directive provides for amendments to the Act on Combating Unfair Commercial Practices and the Consumer Rights Act. The government’s description of the draft bill indicates that the new regulations are intended to make it easier to identify reliable product information and to limit the use of misleading claims regarding the environmental impact of products.

The most practical change concerns general environmental claims. Businesses will not be able to freely use terms such as ‘ecological’, ‘green’ or ‘environmentally friendly’ unless they can substantiate them with reliable evidence. It will also be prohibited to present a product as more environmentally beneficial than it actually is, or to suggest environmental benefits without proper justification.

In practice, this marks the end of an approach where a green leaf, soft-coloured packaging and a few general words were deemed sufficient to convey a product’s ‘eco’ credentials. Once the new regulations come into force, environmental communication will require greater rigour in terms of evidence. Environmental claims should be specific, verifiable and defensible.

## Environmental labels under greater scrutiny

The draft also provides for greater credibility of environmental labels. Labels and marks indicating a product’s environmental impact may only be used if they are based on a recognised certification scheme or have been established by a public body. The aim is to limit the number of proprietary, unverified labels that may give the impression of independent certification, whilst in reality they are merely a marketing tool.

This is a significant change for businesses that use their own graphics, pictograms, quality marks, environmental icons or labels suggesting a lower environmental impact of their products. The issue will not be limited to text alone. Risks may also arise from the graphic design — particularly if the average consumer might perceive a given label as confirming an objective, verified characteristic of the product.

Therefore, verifying communications against EmpCo requirements should not be limited to advertising slogans. It is necessary to check the entire packaging, product information sheet, website, online marketplace, adverts, sales materials, in-store labelling and B2B2C communications. Greenwashing rarely occurs in a single sentence. More often, it is the result of the overall impression a brand builds around a product.

## Durability, reparability and consumer information

The EmpCo does not apply solely to green claims. The new regulations are also intended to improve the information provided to consumers regarding the durability and reparability of products. Before making a purchase, consumers are to receive more information on whether a product can be repaired and to what extent this is possible. In the case of products with digital components, consumers are to be informed about the period during which updates will be provided.

The government’s description of the draft legislation also highlights greater transparency regarding guarantees. Consumers are to receive clear information about their statutory rights, whilst any additional durability guarantee offered by the manufacturer is to be presented in a standardised and easily recognisable manner.

In practice, this means that the implementation of EmpCo may not be limited to the marketing department. In many companies, it will require collaboration between legal, compliance, quality, procurement, R&D, customer service, e-commerce and those responsible for labelling or packaging.

## When will the regulations come into force?

Member States were required to transpose the EmpCo Directive by 27 March 2026, and the national regulations are to apply from 27 September 2026. The government’s description of the Polish draft bill indicates the same date of entry into force.

This means that businesses can no longer afford to wait. Even if the exact wording of national legislation still requires monitoring of the legislative process, the direction of the changes is clear. Environmental communication is set to become more precise, more verifiable and less prone to marketing shortcuts.

## What should businesses do now?

The first step should be to review environmental claims. All slogans, labels and messages concerning the environmental impact of a product, its packaging or the business itself should be compiled. This concerns not only obvious declarations such as ‘eco’, ‘green’ or ‘climate-neutral’, but also more subtle suggestions: graphics, in-house certifications, icons, comparisons, and claims regarding recycling, emissions, biodegradability, composition, the origin of raw materials or the environmental footprint.

The second step is to provide evidence to support each claim. If a business claims that a product has a lower environmental impact, it should be able to specify: lower than what, to what extent, at what stage of the product’s life cycle, according to which methodology, and based on what data. A general assertion that the product is ‘more environmentally friendly’ will not suffice.

The third step is to verify labels and certificates. If a company uses an environmental label, it must be established whether it is based on a recognised certification scheme or has been established by a public body. Proprietary labels that resemble independent certificates may become one of the riskiest areas following the introduction of new regulations.

The fourth step is to streamline the approval process for marketing communications. In practice, many greenwashing risks arise not because a company wishes to mislead consumers, but because the marketing, design, sales and legal departments operate on different versions of reality. EmpCo requires that environmental communications be checked before publication, rather than only once they have attracted the attention of competitors, a consumer organisation or the Director-General of the Office of Competition and Consumer Protection (UOKiK).

## EmpCo as a test of environmental communication maturity

The new regulations do not prohibit providing information about the environmental characteristics of products. However, they do prohibit providing such information in a vague, exaggerated or unsubstantiated manner. This is a significant difference. Businesses will still be able to talk about genuine environmental measures, certifications, improvements to packaging, reductions in emissions or the use of recycled materials. However, they will have to do so with precision.

The EmpCo may therefore bring order to the market, but for many companies it will also represent a painful break with their existing marketing language. Particularly vulnerable will be sectors in which ‘naturalness’, ‘eco-friendliness’, ‘sustainability’ or ‘environmental responsibility’ are a key element of product positioning: food, cosmetics, household chemicals, clothing, packaging, homeware, electronics, e-commerce and children’s products.
